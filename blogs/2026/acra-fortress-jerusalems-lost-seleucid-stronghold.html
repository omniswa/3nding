Acra Fortress: Jerusalem's Lost Seleucid Stronghold

By omniswa · September 3, 2026 · 5 min read

What Was the Acra Fortress?

The Acra fortress was a military citadel built by Seleucid king Antiochus IV Epiphanes in Jerusalem around 168–167 BCE. It housed a Greek garrison and Hellenized Jewish allies, and its job was simple: control the city and keep an eye on the Temple Mount.

For over a century, nobody knew exactly where it stood. That changed in 2015, when archaeologists working in the City of David's Givati Parking Lot uncovered what most experts now accept as its remains.

This wasn't just another guard post. The Acra shaped the outcome of one of the most consequential religious rebellions in ancient history — the Maccabean Revolt.

Why Antiochus IV Built It

Antiochus IV didn't build the Acra on a whim. He'd just sacked Jerusalem, and he needed a way to hold it.

Jerusalem in the 2nd century BCE was split. One faction wanted to adopt Greek customs and integrate into the wider Hellenistic world. The other, centered on traditionalist priests, wanted no part of it.

Antiochus backed the Hellenizers. The fortress was staffed by Greek mercenaries and Hellenized Jews, who many historians believe were locked in a low-grade civil war with the traditionalist Jews who would later rally around the Maccabees. The Acra gave the Greek side a permanent, fortified base right in the middle of that conflict.

Its location made that possible. The stronghold sat on a high bedrock cliff overlooking the steep slopes of the City of David. From there, it controlled every approach to the Temple Mount. Anyone wanting to reach the Temple had to get past it first.

Life Inside the Fortress

The Acra wasn't a small outpost. Excavations turned up a real defensive complex.

Feature Detail
Tower base Roughly 13 x 66 feet
Wall system Massive fortification wall coated in plaster
Glacis An artificial defensive slope built to slow attackers
Garrison Greek soldiers and Hellenized Jewish allies
Roofing Ceramic tile roofs, a building technique imported from Seleucid Syria

That tile detail matters more than it sounds. Roof tiles were a new technology in Jerusalem at the time, and their presence traces directly back to the fortress's construction. It's a small but concrete sign of how thoroughly Greek this outpost was, right down to the building materials.

Weapons finds back up the military picture. Archaeologists recovered lead sling shots, bronze arrowheads, and ballista stones at the site — physical evidence of the fighting that took place around its walls.

The Acra's Role in the Maccabean Revolt

The Acra wasn't a side detail in the Hanukkah story. It was a main character.

The fortress existed to consolidate Seleucid control over Jerusalem, and it played a central role throughout the Maccabean revolt against Greek rule. Judah Maccabee could win battles and even reclaim the Temple, but as long as the Acra stood, a hostile garrison sat directly above it, watching everything.

That standoff dragged on for decades. The fortress didn't fall during the early rebellion. It outlasted Judah Maccabee himself.

The end came under his brother. Simon Maccabeus finally took the Acra in 141 BCE after a long siege, essentially starving the Greek garrison into submission. No dramatic breach, no final battle. Just attrition until the defenders had nothing left.

Simon reportedly had the fortress razed afterward, erasing the symbol of foreign control it represented. That's a big part of why finding physical evidence of it took archaeologists so long.

How Archaeologists Finally Found the Acra

The Acra's exact location was one of Jerusalem archaeology's oldest unsolved questions.

The fortress is mentioned in both books of Maccabees and by the historian Josephus, but the physical archaeological record from the Seleucid period in Jerusalem was thin — which is exactly what made pinpointing the site so difficult. Theories piled up for a hundred years without a confirmed answer.

The breakthrough came in November 2015. Israel Antiquities Authority archaeologists Doron Ben-Ami, Yana Tchekhanovets, and Salome Cohen, excavating the Givati Parking Lot next to the City of David, announced they'd uncovered a complex of rooms and fortified walls matching the Acra.

The evidence lined up on multiple fronts:

  • Coins: A run of coins spanning the reigns of Antiochus IV through Antiochus VII gave the site a clear date range.
  • Imports: A large number of wine amphorae imported from the Aegean pointed to non-Jewish residents living there.
  • Military branding: Bronze arrowheads stamped with trident symbols tied directly to Antiochus IV's reign.

Researchers described it as a solution to one of the great archaeological riddles in the history of Jerusalem. The site has since been developed for public visits as part of the City of David national park.

Acra Fortress: Quick Facts

  • Built by: Antiochus IV Epiphanes
  • Date built: Around 168–167 BCE
  • Purpose: Military control of Jerusalem and the Temple Mount
  • Garrison: Seleucid soldiers and Hellenized Jews
  • Fell to: Simon Maccabeus, 141 BCE, after a prolonged siege
  • Rediscovered: 2015, Givati Parking Lot, City of David

The Bottom Line

The Acra fortress was Antiochus IV's answer to a rebellious city — a fortified garrison built to choke off access to the Temple and prop up Hellenistic control of Jerusalem. It held that ground for roughly 26 years before Simon Maccabeus starved it into surrender in 141 BCE.

For over a century, historians only had ancient texts to go on. Now, thanks to the 2015 excavation in the City of David, the Acra is no longer a mystery. It's a documented, walkable piece of Jerusalem's past.
